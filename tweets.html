Part II · Contagion news

Following the pandemic in real time, one newspaper tweet at a time

We tracked every COVID-19 tweet posted by major newspapers in 20 countries and assigned each newspaper its headquarters time zone. Tweets measure how news spreads, and retweets and likes measure attention. Together they give us a contagion-news factor that can be measured by the hour.

Information diffusion

News spikes when a country's epidemic starts

Countries enter their epidemic phase at very different dates. We mark each one's start as the day it reaches 100 confirmed cases. In almost every country, the number of tweets and the attention they get (retweets, shown by marker size) jump right after that date.

Intraday

Much of the news breaks while markets are open

In local time, tweet volume in every half-hour slot goes up sharply in the first wave compared with the pre-epidemic period, and much of it lands during trading hours. Following newspapers on social media therefore tracks what investors know, in real time.

The contagion factor

Measuring the tone of the news

The tweets come in many languages, so we score them with Polyglot, which has polarity lexicons for 136 languages. Each tweet's tone follows Twedt & Rees (2012):

tone = (#positive − #negative) / (#positive + #negative)

We average tone within each day and across countries. News is the gap between today's tone and its 5-day backward-looking average, which is close to serially uncorrelated. The global tone matches the epidemic's path: it fell sharply as China's outbreak worsened in late January, recovered in early February, fell again when Italy's epidemic began, and then improved slowly as contagion curves flattened.

We also track the quote-retweets of top-1% high-attention tweets. Their tone is positively and significantly correlated with the tone of the original tweets.

Asset pricing

The market price of viral contagion news

An LDA topic model on English-language tweets shows that case counts are one of the main things the news talks about. So every day we sort countries into High, Medium, and Low portfolios by their share of COVID-19 cases, separately for advanced and emerging economies. Each portfolio's exposure to global news varies over time with that share.

rexf,t+1 = r̄exf,t + βf,t · newsglobt+1,     βf,t = β0 + βf,1 Xf,t,     ∂r̄exf,t / ∂Xf,t = λ βf,1

Xf,t = portfolio f's share of contagion cases; λ = market price of risk of global news. Global news is measured either as surprises in the growth of global cases or as surprises in global tweet tone. Estimated by GMM.

≥ 0.3%

A reasonable lower bound on the daily market price of risk of contagion news, after accounting for estimation uncertainty.

Case share predicts returns

A higher share of contagion cases forecasts higher expected returns, so it raises the cost of capital.

Survives the controls

Holds after controlling for the global market, FF3/FF5, jumps, and realized volatility, with USD or local-currency returns, and in hourly data with country fixed effects.

Why so large, yet plausible? This isn't the price of a traded financial factor, and the estimated betas are small. Contagion risk also comes in short-lived waves, so assets' exposure to it is modest and fades quickly. A conditional CAPM using market returns instead of COVID news finds no significant positive price of risk.
In real time

Risk premia on a High-minus-Low COVID strategy

We re-estimate the model on samples of increasing length, starting in June 2020 and adding two weeks at a time. This shows how early an investor using our method could have measured contagion exposure. The market price of risk is significant in almost every window.

+28% / +13%

Possible rise in daily equity risk premia at the April 2020 peak, advanced and emerging economies, relative to sample medians.

Flows agree

High-COVID countries see weekly equity outflows. In emerging economies, flows into government bonds rise, consistent with a flight to safety.

Energy > Health

Across STOXX Europe 600 industries, Energy, Utilities, and Consumer Discretionary are the most exposed. Health, Materials, and IT are the safest.

Policy implication. Containment policies that keep relative contagion low could be very valuable, both for lives saved and for protecting global financial wealth.
